# Headlines, leads and hooks

Sources: Agora (notes 16, 18: Bly, MMF and Forde's *Great Leads*, Hunt, Ogilvy's 38 rules); Hormozi's Hooks and GOATed Ads (note 06).

## Why it matters
- Ogilvy: 5× as many people read the headline as the body. "After you've written your headline, you've spent eighty cents of your advertising dollar."
- Hormozi: with 10 hours to make an ad, spend 8 on hooks. A good hook can lift attention 5–100× (his claim).
- **Dean Graziosi.** Re-shooting only the opener, from an ego-led "multi-NYT bestseller…" to "Have you ever looked in the mirror and thought 'I should be further ahead by now'?", turned a flop into a hit. **Make the reader feel important, not the speaker.**

## Four U's (MMF via Bly): score every headline 1–4 on each
**Urgent · Useful · Unique · Ultra-specific.** Great headlines sometimes break the rules by creating a cinematic picture and curiosity instead.

## Hook = call-out + condition for value (Hormozi)
- **Call-out:** "this is for me" (the cocktail-party effect).
- **Condition for value:** "If [reader] [does this], they get [reward]."
- Make it specific enough to get the right people and broad enough to get all of them.
- **Hook types:**
  - labels ("Dubai residents…", "Hotel GMs in Deira…");
  - questions;
  - conditionals ("If more than half your rooms come through OTAs…");
  - commands ("Read this before you book Eid");
  - statements;
  - lists ("5 things…");
  - stories;
  - exclamations.
  - Non-verbal hooks: contrast, the scene, landmarks.
- **70/20/10:** 70% proven hooks (keep a swipe file with results), 20% variations on winners, 10% wild tests. Losers are logged too.

## Bly's headline shortcuts (the ones that fit hotels and B2B)
- Ask a question.
- Tie in to current events (sourced, same day).
- Sound like news ("Announcing…", "New:").
- Use numbers.
- "How to…" (promise useful information).
- Highlight the offer.
- Tell a story.
- State the benefit.
- Make a comparison.
- Create a visual image.
- Use a testimonial (real).
- Offer a free report or guide.
- Arouse curiosity.
- Be specific (the Rolls-Royce clock).
- Target a particular reader.
- Add a time element.
- Stress savings or value.
- Give good news.
- Stress the guarantee.
- Make a contradictory promise ("A beach holiday, without the flight").

**Localise** (Ogilvy #30): put the city or area name in local ads. **Flag your prospect** (#31): "Families in Abu Dhabi:". **Long headlines (10+ words) sold more** in mail order (Ogilvy, 1980s). Test this; don't assume it.

## The six lead types (MMF and Forde): pick by awareness
Direct (aware end): **Offer, Promise, Problem-Solution.** Indirect (unaware end): **Secret, Proclamation, Story.**
1. **Offer lead.** The most emotionally compelling detail of the deal → its best benefit → elaborate → **a reason why** you're making it (answer "what's the catch?" honestly). Most-aware readers respond to price; less-aware ones to trials, premiums and guarantees.
2. **Promise lead.** The biggest *provable* claim first. The best promises are about how readers will feel or be seen. If a promise feels too big, make it a question.
3. **Problem-Solution lead.** What keeps them up at night → make sure it has emotional weight → stir it with empathy → offer hope.
4. **Secret lead.** A little-known (true) feature, or a familiar one renamed. Tease, drop clues, and reveal only when they're emotionally ready.
5. **Proclamation lead.** A bold, *researched* statement that implies a promise. Return to it at the close.
6. **Story lead.** An ordinary hero, an extraordinary attempt, the doubters proved wrong. Readers forget it's an ad. It must be a *true* story.

**Boots on the ground** (Brian Hunt) is the most credible lead: "I was there." For a hotel, that's a real guest's moment or a scene the writer actually saw. For B2B, it's what Saif saw at the property.

**Leads to avoid:**
- the boring personal anecdote with a flimsy link;
- the company-history opener ("Founded in 1998…");
- describing your process.

## Bly's persuasion moves for leads
- **"So what?" test:** rewrite until the reaction is "That's exactly what I want. How do I get it?"
- **Move your strongest point (often your last line) to the first line.**
- **No obvious lead:** open mid-conversation.
- **Communicate trust first.**
- Copy should be "dripping" with one driver: fear, greed, guilt, exclusivity, anger, salvation or flattery. For hotels, the usual ones are exclusivity, belonging, relief and indulgence.

## Fear vs greed (Agora, 2007 debate)
Lead with the opportunity and address the fear without stating it (Palmer). A pitch built only on loss-avoidance goes "watery", and one built only on promises goes stale. **[MH]** For GMs: opportunity (direct revenue) plus the unstated fear (OTA dependency, owner pressure).

## Email subject lines (Hormozi's tested set; adapt the structure, not the claims)
"Btw… (a favour to ask)" · "Because you [thing they just did]" · "Thank you (here's a private invite)" · "Only open this if [condition]" · "[Hotel]'s direct bookings" (MetaNex) · "Found something on [Hotel]'s rates" (needs a real finding).

**Dean Jackson's 9-word email:** "Are you still looking to [4-word desire]?" No links.

## News headlines (Bonner)
News alone isn't enough. Add an **opinion** or a **benefit**: "Dubai's summer occupancy dips: why direct bookers are winning". The data must be sourced.

## Brainstorm rule (MMF)
Great ideas are 80% obvious and 20% twist, and the twist can't be summarised. Capture phrases, not summaries. Produce a headline plus lead within 24–48 hours, and treat 80% right as good enough to Peer-review.
